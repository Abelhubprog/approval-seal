approval-seal

Sealed, expiring, single-use approvals for AI agent actions, plus a receipt checker. Take the 2 minute guided demo, check receipts against Hyperliquid's public record, or verify a record yourself. Everything runs in your browser. No login, no server of ours. Source on GitHub.

EXAMPLE DATA: the order, the approver and the approval in steps 1 to 6 are made up and generated in your browser. No trade is placed.

Step 1 of 7: Agent proposes

    1. Agent proposes

    An AI agent wants to buy a little ETH on Hyperliquid. It writes the order down as plain data. Nothing is signed and nothing is sent anywhere.

    EXAMPLE DATA: this order is made up for the demo. It is not a real trade and is never sent to any venue.
    What this provesOnly what the agent asked for, in a form that can be hashed.
    What it does not proveThat anyone has agreed to it. This is a proposal, not a trade.

    2. Owner approves

    You click approve. approval-seal binds this exact order to the approval with a SHA-256 seal. The seal is stored separately, the way an approval service would keep it.

    What this provesThe order, the approver name, the time, the nonce and the expiry are fixed as of this moment.
    What it does not proveWho approved. The approver is just a name in version 0.1.0. There is no signature or key.

    3. Anyone verifies

    Anyone holding the record and the stored seal can recompute the hashes in their own browser and check them.

    What this provesThe record is unchanged since it was sealed, it matches the stored seal, and it has not expired.
    What it does not proveWho approved, whether the trade is a good idea, or whether it is safe or profitable.

    4. Tamper

    Now an attacker edits the approved order after the fact. Pick one change. Each button edits a copy of the record and verifies it again.

    What this provesAny edit to the approved order is caught, and the check names the field that changed.
    What it does not proveThat the executor sends the approved order. Your executor must send exactly the approved action and nothing else.

    5. Forge attempt

    A smarter attacker changes the amount and also recomputes the hashes so the record looks consistent. Does it get through?

    What this provesA forged record can look self-consistent, but it cannot match the seal that was stored at approval time.
    What it does not proveProtection if the attacker can also overwrite the stored seal. Keep it where they cannot write.

    6. Replay

    An approval is single use. The executor claims it once to run the order. What if the same approval is used a second time?

    What this provesThe same approver and nonce cannot be claimed twice by executors that share one store.
    What it does not proveReplay protection across machines. This demo store lives in your tab. Real use needs a shared database constraint.

    7. Check the public record

    The sealed order maps to a Hyperliquid client order id, so a public order can point back to its approval. This step derives that id, then runs the same receipts check as the Hyperliquid receipts tab, against real public orders.

    EXAMPLE DATA: the receipts below are real public orders from a third-party wallet. They are not PROWZI receipts and were not made with approval-seal.
    What this provesHyperliquid's own public API reports filled orders with those ids for that wallet. No login.
    What it does not proveWho approved a trade, that it was a good idea, or that it was safe or profitable. These are not PROWZI receipts.
    That is the whole idea. Any change to an approved action is caught, forged records fail against the stored seal, and each approval works once. The seal proves the record is unchanged since sealing. It does not prove who approved, and says nothing about profit or safety.
